The Bloch sphere: a short primer

1. One qubit, one point
Every pure qubit state can be written, up to a global phase, as
|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^(iφ)·sin(θ/2)|1⟩. The two angles are exactly the two
angles that locate a point on a sphere of radius 1:

    x = sin θ · cos φ      y = sin θ · sin φ      z = cos θ

θ is the tilt away from the top (0° to 180°) and φ is the turn around the
vertical axis (0° to 360°). The arrow from the centre to this point is the
Bloch vector r = (x, y, z).

2. Six landmarks
    |0⟩  → (0, 0, 1)  top            |1⟩  → (0, 0, −1)  bottom
    |+⟩  → (1, 0, 0)                 |−⟩  → (−1, 0, 0)
    |+i⟩ → (0, 1, 0)                 |−i⟩ → (0, −1, 0)
The four superpositions sit on the equator (θ = 90°); they differ only in φ.

3. A worked example
θ = 60°, φ = 90°: x = sin 60°·cos 90° = 0, y = sin 60°·sin 90° = 0.866,
z = cos 60° = 0.5. The point is (0, 0.866, 0.5).

4. Height is probability
The height z alone fixes the computational-basis probabilities:

    P(0) = (1 + z)/2        P(1) = (1 − z)/2

For the example, P(0) = (1 + 0.5)/2 = 0.75 and P(1) = 0.25. The angle φ does
not appear: turning a state around the vertical axis changes its relative
phase but not P(0) or P(1). All equator states have z = 0, so P(0) = 0.5.

5. Opposite points are orthogonal states
Two states that are orthogonal (⟨φ|ψ⟩ = 0) sit at opposite ends of a line
through the centre: |0⟩ and |1⟩, |+⟩ and |−⟩, |+i⟩ and |−i⟩. Careful: the
angle between two arrows on the sphere is twice the angle between the state
vectors. That is why the formula uses θ/2: |0⟩ and |1⟩ are 180° apart on the
sphere although they are perpendicular (90°) as vectors.

6. Reading the point from a density matrix
Every one-qubit density matrix is ρ = ½(I + x·σx + y·σy + z·σz), so

    x = 2·Re(ρ₀₁)      y = −2·Im(ρ₀₁)      z = ρ₀₀ − ρ₁₁

z comes from the diagonal (the probabilities); x and y come from the
off-diagonal entry (the coherence).
Example: ρ = [[0.8, 0.2], [0.2, 0.2]] gives x = 0.4, y = 0, z = 0.6.

7. Surface or inside
The length of the Bloch vector is |r| = √(x² + y² + z²). Pure states have
|r| = 1 (on the surface); mixed states have |r| < 1 (inside); the maximally
mixed state I/2 is the centre, |r| = 0. Purity and length are linked by
Tr(ρ²) = ½(1 + |r|²).
For the example, |r|² = 0.16 + 0 + 0.36 = 0.52, so |r| ≈ 0.72 < 1: the state
is mixed, and Tr(ρ²) = ½(1 + 0.52) = 0.76.
