Measuring in other bases: a short primer

1. A measurement asks one yes/no question
Measuring a qubit means picking two orthogonal states and asking "which of
these two?". The pair is called a basis. So far the pair was always |0⟩, |1⟩
(the computational basis, also called the Z basis). Any other orthonormal
pair works just as well.

2. The rule
For a basis |b₀⟩, |b₁⟩ and a state |ψ⟩:

    P(b₀) = |⟨b₀|ψ⟩|²        P(b₁) = |⟨b₁|ψ⟩|²

The inner product ⟨b|ψ⟩ says how much of |ψ⟩ points along |b⟩; its squared
size is the probability. The two probabilities add up to 1. After the
measurement the qubit IS the state that was found (|b₀⟩ or |b₁⟩).

3. The X basis: |+⟩ and |−⟩
    |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2        |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2
For |ψ⟩ = a|0⟩ + b|1⟩ with real a, b:
    ⟨+|ψ⟩ = (a + b)/√2        ⟨−|ψ⟩ = (a − b)/√2
Example: |ψ⟩ = 0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩.
    ⟨+|ψ⟩ = 1.4/√2 = 0.990, so P(+) = 0.98
    ⟨−|ψ⟩ = −0.2/√2 = −0.141, so P(−) = 0.02
In the computational basis the same state gives P(0) = 0.36, P(1) = 0.64.
One state, two different questions, two different sets of probabilities.

4. Why the basis matters
|+⟩ and |−⟩ both give P(0) = P(1) = 0.5 in the computational basis, so that
measurement cannot tell them apart. In the X basis |+⟩ gives "+" with
certainty and |−⟩ gives "−" with certainty. The relative phase, invisible in
one basis, decides everything in the other.

5. Measuring in the X basis with a gate
A device that only measures 0/1 can still measure in the X basis: apply H
first, then measure. H turns |+⟩ into |0⟩ and |−⟩ into |1⟩, so "0" means "+"
and "1" means "−". Check: H(0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩) = 0.990|0⟩ − 0.141|1⟩, which
gives P(0) = 0.98 - the same number as P(+) above.

6. The Y basis
    |+i⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2        |−i⟩ = (|0⟩ − i|1⟩)/√2
A bra takes complex conjugates: ⟨+i| = (⟨0| − i⟨1|)/√2. For
0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩: ⟨+i|ψ⟩ = (0.6 − 0.8i)/√2, whose squared size is
(0.36 + 0.64)/2 = 0.5. So P(+i) = P(−i) = 0.5.

7. The picture on the Bloch sphere
Every basis is one axis through the centre of the sphere; its two states are
the two ends of that axis. If n is the unit vector pointing to |b₀⟩ and r is
the Bloch vector of the state:

    P(b₀) = (1 + r·n)/2        P(b₁) = (1 − r·n)/2

r·n is the shadow of the arrow on the axis. For the three standard axes:
    Z basis: P(0)  = (1 + z)/2
    X basis: P(+)  = (1 + x)/2
    Y basis: P(+i) = (1 + y)/2
Example: 0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩ has r = (0.96, 0, −0.28). P(+) = (1 + 0.96)/2 = 0.98,
P(0) = (1 − 0.28)/2 = 0.36, P(+i) = (1 + 0)/2 = 0.5 - all as computed above.

8. With a density matrix
For any state ρ, pure or mixed: P(b) = ⟨b|ρ|b⟩. The Bloch formula in section 7
holds for mixed states too.
Example: ρ = [[0.8, 0.2], [0.2, 0.2]] has r = (0.4, 0, 0.6).
P(+) = (1 + 0.4)/2 = 0.7 and P(−) = 0.3, while P(0) = 0.8.
The maximally mixed state I/2 has r = (0, 0, 0): it gives 50/50 in every
basis.
